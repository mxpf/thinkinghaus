A System for Looking

August 5, 2026

3 minutes

Before I make anything, I look.

This sounds obvious. It took me a while to understand what I meant by it.

I used to confuse looking with collecting. I would open a browser, save fifty images, and call the result research. The collection grew. My understanding did not.

Now I try to begin with a question.

What am I actually trying to understand? What has become so familiar that no one sees it anymore? What does a person need to know, feel, or do—and in what order?

The question does not need to point toward an answer. It only needs to give the looking a shape.

I start near the subject. If I am working on an annual report, I look at annual reports. I need to understand the form before I decide what to do with it.

Then I move farther away.

I might look at newspapers, maps, instruction manuals, or paintings. A newspaper knows how to handle density. A map knows how to hold several scales at once. A painting may know something useful about directing the eye.

I am not looking for a style to borrow. I am trying to notice what something is doing.

When I save a reference, I write down why. The scale makes the ordinary feel important. The repetition creates trust. The awkward pause makes me pay attention.

That sentence is usually more useful than the image. It gives me enough distance to carry the idea somewhere else without copying its appearance.

Then I arrange what I have found and start removing things.

One reference tells me what I like. Several references placed together begin to tell me what I think. The one that does not fit is often worth keeping around. It may be pointing to something the rest of the collection has politely avoided.

Time helps too. I am easily persuaded by novelty and finish. If I leave the references alone for a day, some become less convincing. A few become more interesting.

Eventually I make something, usually before I feel ready. A rough sketch shows whether an observation can survive contact with actual words, scale, and use. Often it cannot. Then I know where to look next.

So the system is not very complicated: begin with a question, look outside the category, name what matters, arrange, remove, make, and look again.

The point is not to build an impressive moodboard. It is to notice enough that a decision becomes easier.
