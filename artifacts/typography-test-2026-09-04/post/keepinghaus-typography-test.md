I wanted to know what a typeface does to Keepinghaus after it stops being a typeface and becomes the place where the words live.

That sounds like a small distinction, but I think it explains why choosing type from specimens can be so misleading. A specimen asks you to look at the letters. A site asks the letters to help you look at everything else.

The question was whether Charter still felt right when compared with Untitled Serif, Untitled Sans, and an ordinary browser serif across the actual site: the index, a normal essay, a longer essay, a link-heavy page, a list, dates, underlined links, and an image interrupting the prose.

Nothing else changed. Same words. Same widths. Same 16/24 rhythm on desktop and 18/28 on mobile. Same dark brown surface, muted text, spacing, hierarchy, and browser. I did not adjust the measure to flatter any font. The differences in line breaks and page length are what the fonts did on their own.

![Four versions of the Keepinghaus index at the same desktop viewport](/images/keepinghaus-typography-01-index-desktop.webp "Charter, Untitled Serif, Untitled Sans, and the browser serif on the same index")

## Ordinary is not the same as inconspicuous

Untitled Sans was the comparison I was most curious about. It was designed in pursuit of the quotidian: ordinary, adaptable, non-special. Look at the letterforms by themselves and the description makes sense. There is little visible flourish and almost nothing asking to be admired.

Inside Keepinghaus, it does not disappear.

It is actually the typeface I notice fastest. Its even texture, large apparent x-height, and familiar contemporary neutrality turn out to be powerful signals. The dates begin to resemble interface labels. The index becomes cleaner and more utilitarian. The essays feel a little more like a design publication or portfolio whose restraint has been art-directed.

The face contains less obvious character, but the choice creates more design presence.

![The same prose page in all four conditions at a desktop viewport](/images/keepinghaus-typography-02-prose-desktop.webp "A direct desktop comparison on Personal folklore")

Part of this belongs to the letters. Part belongs to the rest of the page. Keepinghaus has a warm dark palette, lots of negative space, close-to-equal type sizes, and the proportions of a document rather than an interface. Put a restrained sans inside that language and the contrast becomes expressive. The ostensibly neutral decision starts to look like a decision about neutrality.

There are cultural associations too. Contemporary grotesks have become a fluent dialect of portfolios, institutions, products, and independent publishing. A person does not need to recognize Untitled Sans to recognize the world around it.

This is not a criticism of the typeface. On the index and links page it is the clearest thing here. It scans beautifully. But the ease of scanning comes with a change in genre. The site feels newly built and carefully maintained instead of slowly inhabited.

## Two kinds of serif

Untitled Serif is the serious alternative.

It reads extremely well. It is slightly more compact than Charter at the same measure and maintains a calm, even page. On a long essay it gives the prose a polished continuity without the genre change introduced by Sans.

But its refinement keeps surfacing. The shapes feel composed. Italics are notably graceful. Even the small “Last edited” line starts to feel like an editorial detail rather than the ordinary evidence that somebody came back and changed something.

![The same prose page in all four conditions at a mobile viewport](/images/keepinghaus-typography-03-prose-mobile.webp "The typefaces compared at 390 by 844 pixels")

With Untitled Serif, Keepinghaus becomes more publication-like. Not dramatically. It is still quiet, still a web page, still mostly words. But the hand of the designer becomes easier to see. The plainness begins to feel designed *as plainness*.

Charter is not characterless either. It brings a familiar literary and slightly old-web tone. That is the strongest argument against it. On the index, especially, it can make the site feel a little more like a reading room than an ordinary page.

In the prose, though, that familiarity becomes useful. I notice Charter and then stop noticing it. The typeface has enough warmth to keep the dark page from becoming austere, but not so much polish that the typography becomes an object. The unevenness feels human without turning quaint. The site seems tended.

## The bottom of the test

The browser serif was there to establish what minimally authored typography feels like. It makes the shortest pages and puts the greatest number of words on each line. Individual letters recede almost immediately.

But defaultness is not invisible either.

The page starts to feel generic, nostalgic, or unfinished. The rest of the design is still present—the measure, the brown-black surface, the spacing—but some of its care no longer registers as care. Plainness has stopped looking chosen.

That was useful to see. Keepinghaus probably does not want invisible design. Invisible design is too easy to confuse with the absence of design. What seems more promising is care that remains perceptible while its authorship stays quiet.

![A link-heavy page in all four typography conditions](/images/keepinghaus-typography-04-links-desktop.webp "Persistent underlined links reveal different degrees of editorial and interface character")

I would not call that a principle yet. This is one implementation, one palette, one body of writing. On a white page, at a wider measure, or in a site made mostly of short fragments, the result might reverse. Here, though, the distinction holds.

## What the page became

The differences were clearest when the content stayed still and the surrounding genre changed.

Charter made the site feel like a cared-for personal place. Untitled Serif made it feel like an independent literary publication. Untitled Sans made it feel like a contemporary design-led publishing system. The browser serif made it feel like an old or minimally styled web document.

![The same list-heavy page in all four conditions on mobile](/images/keepinghaus-typography-05-list-mobile.webp "The Now page tests lists, dates, links, and long mobile wrapping")

The image page made the same thing visible in another way. The illustration never changed. With Untitled Sans it began to read like portfolio work placed in a designed sequence. With Untitled Serif it became an illustration in an essay publication. With the browser serif it looked inserted into an old document. Charter gave it the least categorical frame. It was simply something the page had arrived at.

![The same image in context on the long prose page](/images/keepinghaus-typography-06-media-desktop.webp "Typography changes how the unchanged image is framed")

The metric differences were real but not decisive. In the normal desktop essay, Charter produced a 1,548-pixel page. Untitled Serif came in at 1,500, Untitled Sans at 1,596, and the browser serif at 1,476. On the long mobile essay, Untitled Sans added about six percent to Charter’s page length. That extra scrolling matches its more assertive texture, but page length alone does not determine reading comfort.

![The complete long page in all four conditions](/images/keepinghaus-typography-07-longform-full-desktop.webp "Whole-page rhythm and relative page length across the four conditions")

## What survived

Charter survived the test.

Not because it is the prettiest face, and not because the alternatives have too much character. “Too much character” now seems like the wrong diagnosis. Untitled Sans demonstrates why: it has less formal character and more perceived design presence.

The more useful question is what relationship a typeface creates between care and authorship.

The browser serif hides too much care. Untitled Sans makes authorship too visible. Untitled Serif comes close, but tips the site toward a composed editorial identity. Charter holds the middle. The page feels looked after, while the reason for looking after it remains the material being kept there.
