Jim Nielsen recently wrote that [“carelessness is the grain of AI.”](https://blog.jim-nielsen.com/2026/fruit-of-ai/) It got under my skin because it sounded true, and because Thinkinghaus is co-authored with AI. This essay is no exception.

Nielsen was responding to [Terry Godier’s apology](https://blog.terrygodier.com/2026/08/09/mea-culpa-dark-hours.html) for Dark Hours, an astronomy website he built using Claude. After launching it, Godier learned that his project was strikingly similar to an existing open-source project called [DarkHours](https://github.com/mbeher2200/DarkHours), right down to reproducing a bug its creator, Miguel Beher, had already fixed. Godier killed his project, redirected the domain to Beher’s, and said plainly that he had relied on AI without doing enough work to understand what he was publishing.

The detail that keeps bothering me is that Beher’s original DarkHours also credits GitHub Copilot and Claude. Both projects were made with help from some of the same tools. Beher’s project also has hundreds of commits, extensive documentation, an explanation of its scientific methods, and careful acknowledgment of its data sources.

So the presence of AI doesn’t tell me very much by itself.

There was another failure of attention tangled up in the story. John Gruber had written a post excoriating Apple for rejecting Godier’s astronomy app as astrology, only to discover that the app Apple had actually reviewed really had begun as an astrology app. In his [retraction](https://daringfireball.net/2026/08/retraction_app_store_rejection_of_the_week), Gruber is unusually specific about how he got there. He admired Godier’s previous work. He thought very little of astrology. The possibility that the story might be almost exactly backward simply didn’t occur to him. He says it was his first full retraction in 24 years of Daring Fireball.

No AI required. Admiration worked fine.

Nielsen gets the idea of “grain” from Frank Chimero’s [*The Web’s Grain*](https://frankchimero.com/blog/2015/the-webs-grain/), which begins with Chimero trying to look at the web again before years of expertise and disappointment decide what it is. The grain of a material isn’t a moral property. Wood isn’t good because it has grain. The point is to pay attention to what a thing wants to do before you bully it into the shape you brought from home.

I’ve been trying to decide what AI wants to do. Frictionlessness gets close. Amplification gets close too. Both start sounding like something I’d put on a conference slide if I hold onto them for more than a few minutes.

What I know better is what it feels like to use.

There’s simply too much stuff I want to look at. AI can recognize patterns across more material than I could reasonably hold in my head, cross-reference things I wouldn’t have thought to put together, and hand me a connection I can go chase down myself. Sometimes the connection falls apart immediately. Sometimes it’s technically true and completely boring. Sometimes it opens a hole in the floor.

A map is useful here. I like getting lost. I also occasionally need to get somewhere before retirement. Refusing the map because I didn’t personally consider every possible road seems like a strange purity test.

The highway contains the downside. I can cover enormous distances while getting almost no sense of where I am. Stay on one long enough and New Jersey, Ohio, and Indiana start to become exits, concrete barriers, gas stations, and the same cheeseburger under slightly different lighting. AI can do that to knowledge. I can travel very far without ever getting off.

I’m still not going to blame the highway for the towns I chose not to enter.

A telescope does something else. It changes the scale at which I can see. Nobody expects an astronomer to squint harder instead of using Hubble. The instrument reveals things the unaided body can’t. Somebody still has to point it somewhere, understand what comes back, notice what’s strange, doubt the instrument, and decide what deserves another look.

That’s closer to my experience of AI than having a machine make things for me, although sometimes it plainly does make things for me. That’s where this gets harder.

I use AI to research, write, criticize, connect, question, and occasionally articulate something I’ve been circling without being able to quite get my hands around. Every so often it gives me a sentence I couldn’t have written myself and I read it and think: yes. That. That’s what I was trying to say.

I don’t think a pencil does that.

A pencil has properties. Photoshop has assumptions buried all through it. A camera decides plenty before the photographer touches it. But none of them generally tries to understand me, remembers something I said six months ago, pushes back on an argument, or notices that two ideas I’ve been treating separately might actually be the same problem wearing different pants.

Plato worried about writing more than two thousand years ago. In the [*Phaedrus*](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/phaedrus.html), Socrates tells a story about the invention of writing and worries that people will mistake access to externalized knowledge for understanding. Then he makes a stranger complaint: written words resemble something alive, but ask them a question and they just sit there. They can’t explain themselves, adjust to the person asking, or come to their own defense.

The externalized thought answers back now.

J.C.R. Licklider was already poking at something like this in 1960. His [*Man-Computer Symbiosis*](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/medg/people/psz/Licklider) contains a small experiment I love. He tracked what he actually did during the hours he considered thinking and discovered that about 85 percent of the time was spent getting himself into a position to think: finding information, plotting it, calculating things, converting incompatible material into comparable forms. In one example, hours of preparation produced a relationship that became obvious within seconds once the information could finally be seen together.

Even what he chose to investigate was being shaped by what was clerically feasible.

I know that feeling. There are things I’m capable of understanding that I would never have bothered trying to understand because finding and assembling all the pieces would have eaten the afternoon, the week, or the rest of my natural life.

Licklider imagined computers taking on some of that preparatory work while humans supplied goals, questions, hypotheses, evaluation and judgment. He also expected the contributions of human and computer to become difficult to separate.

He was less prescient about where the line would fall. Pattern recognition, relevance, remembering that somebody had done related work somewhere—he still put much of that firmly on the human side. Modern AI has wandered straight into it. Quite often the machine is the one saying: hang on, this weird thing you’re talking about resembles something J.C.R. Licklider wrote in 1960.

Then I go read Licklider.

I have a hard time calling that automation. I also have a hard time calling it a pencil.

Sol LeWitt gets me partway there. Many of his [wall drawings](https://whitney.org/collection/works/25527) exist as instructions that other people execute. The Whitney describes the permanent form of *Wall Drawing #289* as its guidelines and certificate; the people making the drawing choose angles and lengths, so individual realizations vary. Authorship has obviously never required one person to make every mark with his own hand.

But LeWitt’s draftsperson doesn’t usually look up from the wall and say, “I think you’re asking the wrong question.”

AI sometimes does.

I can feel that seam in the work, and I don’t particularly want to hide it. For some people, the fact that Thinkinghaus is co-authored with AI will make the work feel inauthentic. I understand the reaction. There’s an enormous amount of hollow AI-generated material in the world. Pretending disgust with it is simply technophobia would be a convenient way of not looking at what people are actually reacting to.

My experience has also been that AI can add resistance rather than just remove it.

I can make something and ask what’s weak about it. I can ask whether an idea I think is original looks suspiciously familiar. I can ask it to argue with me. It can notice contradictions in my own thinking and hand them back. Over time it becomes a strange reflective surface. The therapy comparison brushes up against this, though I don’t want to push it too far: one useful thing another mind can do is listen long enough to ask the question you weren’t quite able to ask yourself.

The robotic-surgery comparison keeps coming back for a related reason. Surgical robots can scale motion so a larger movement of the surgeon’s hand becomes a much smaller movement of the instrument, and they can filter tremor. The machine’s contribution isn’t necessarily more force or more speed. Sometimes it’s less movement.

Writing a tiny personal blog is obviously not microsurgery. I would prefer not to die on that particular hill.

Still, I recognize the arrangement. I’m trying to express things I don’t completely know how to express yet. Something is in there, badly lit and difficult to describe. Making is mostly the repeated attempt to get it outside myself in a form where I recognize it.

Sometimes AI lets me make a finer movement toward it. Sometimes it hands me something polished so quickly that I almost miss the fact that I haven’t recognized myself in it at all.

That has changed what I mean when I call the result mine. I don’t need to pretend every useful connection, criticism, phrase, or question originated inside me. If another intelligence materially changes the work, I’m comfortable acknowledging that. The seam is real.

Responsibility is harder to share.

Godier could have blamed Claude for producing something too close to Beher’s project. He didn’t. Gruber could have blamed Godier for misleading him. He didn’t do that either. Both ended up having to look at the thing they had chosen to put into the world.

There’s a funny echo of Plato there. His written word needed its author to come to its defense. AI can answer back while I’m making something, which is genuinely new and still a little astonishing to me. Once I publish it, though, I’m the one standing next to it.

That’s where Nielsen’s warning keeps its teeth. AI makes it frighteningly easy to move from an intention to something that looks finished without noticing everything I skipped in between.

It has also helped me see farther, ask better questions, and find things I wouldn’t have found alone. I’m grateful for that.

If I put the result out into the world, I still have to answer for it.
