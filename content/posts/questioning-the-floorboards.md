The first time I encountered Gene Ray’s [Time Cube](https://web.archive.org/web/20150506055228/http://www.timecube.com/index.html), I had the sensation that somebody had pulled up a floorboard I didn’t know was removable.

Ray believed that a single rotation of the Earth contains four simultaneous twenty-four-hour days: midnight, sunrise, midday, and sunset, each beginning its own day from a different “corner.” He called this a 96-hour Cubic Day and spent decades arguing that schools, scientists, religions, and basically the rest of humanity were either too stupid or too corrupt to acknowledge it. I don’t believe in Time Cube. I’m reasonably comfortable with the one-day situation.

But that isn’t really what keeps me coming back to the site. What gets me is the original move. Somewhere along the way Gene Ray looked at one of the most boring, immovable facts of ordinary life—there is one day in a day—and apparently thought: says who? That’s a hell of a thing to question.

The site itself is an enormous accumulation of capital letters, diagrams, colors, accusations, repeated phrases, declarations of genius, arguments with academics, and language that sometimes seems to be trying to break free of grammar entirely. There’s almost no distinction between introduction, evidence, conclusion, insult, footnote, revelation, and the thing Gene remembered fifteen minutes ago. Reading it feels less like reading an argument than standing inside one, and for a while I find that exhilarating.

I grew up with a version of the internet where people made places like this. Not necessarily theories of spacetime, but personal territories that weren’t trying especially hard to become publications, audiences, brands, or businesses. Somebody learned enough HTML to make a room and then filled it with whatever was in his head.

There’s an intimacy to that I still find difficult to describe. I can watch an actor give an extraordinary performance or read a great novelist and feel that I know something true about another person, but I also know there’s a made thing between us. A site like Time Cube feels different. Not unmediated—nothing is—but thinner somehow. Gene Ray didn’t seem to be offering a tasteful representation of Gene Ray. He was putting a lot of the machinery directly on the lawn.

You can hear it in interviews with him, too. The strange thing isn’t that he sounds like a monster or an alien. He sounds like a person who is completely, almost physically committed to something he believes everyone else has failed to see.

That commitment is part of what attracts me, and it’s also where the attraction starts to curdle. Time Cube isn’t merely a harmless private cosmology. The site contains racism, antisemitism, homophobia, dehumanizing insults, and repeated declarations that people who reject Ray’s ideas deserve to die. His certainty eventually leaves nowhere for another person to stand.

That seems important, because it would be very easy for me to turn Ray into a little patron saint of outsider thinking: the glorious crank who refused to accept what the experts told him. The thing I admire in him and the thing that frightens me are almost touching. He questioned the floorboards, then built new ones and nailed them down so hard that nobody, including him, was allowed to question those.

I kept thinking about Benjamín Labatut’s *When We Cease to Understand the World*, which follows scientists and mathematicians who pushed so far into their subjects that the border between discovery, obsession, beauty, terror, and personal collapse gets difficult to see. These are obviously not equivalent stories—Gene Ray was not Heisenberg or Grothendieck—but I recognize the unsettling shape of the question: what happens when a mind follows an idea farther than ordinary consensus can accompany it?

Sometimes the answer is that the consensus was wrong. Sometimes the person wandering away from it is wrong. Usually the difficult part is that you don’t receive a little flag telling you which situation you’re in. That uncertainty may be the thing Ray eventually lost.

There’s another personal site I’ve returned to for about twenty years, Andrew Plotkin’s [Eblong](https://eblong.com/zarf/). Plotkin has accumulated games, essays, books, technical work, odd interests, old pages, unfinished things, and evidence of decades spent following whatever held his attention. His professional biography still points people toward the personal site rather than pretending that part of him doesn’t exist.

I feel the same Oh, there you are sensation on both sites, but the resulting worlds feel completely different. Plotkin’s site keeps opening doors. Time Cube eventually closes them.

That distinction helps me understand what I actually admire about these strange personal corners of the internet. It isn’t weirdness. It isn’t being an outsider. It certainly isn’t being wrong with tremendous confidence. It’s the willingness to put some evidence of your mind into the world before every rough edge has been sanded off.

I have a lot of things that never make it that far: drawings, half-built websites, games, experiments, ideas I’ve become embarrassed by before anybody else had the opportunity. I can be extremely good at imagining the skeptical person who will encounter something I’ve made and immediately see why it’s stupid. That imaginary person has killed a surprising amount of work.

Gene Ray apparently did not have this problem. There’s something almost enviable about making a thing for years because you think it matters, putting it on the internet in enormous red letters, arguing with universities about it, and refusing to wait for somebody more qualified to give you permission.

I don’t want his certainty or a worldview with no exits. But there’s something in that first act I’d like to keep: the willingness to look at something everybody else walks across every day and wonder whether it’s actually holding the house up.

Gene Ray pulled up the first board, and I’m still glad he did.
