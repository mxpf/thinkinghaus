There is a scene in The Odyssey where Eumaeus picks up a puppy and throws it from a mountaintop.

He does it casually.

That is the disturbing part.

Odysseus stops him from throwing another and chooses the puppy who will become Argos. The moment becomes understandable again. We know who to identify with and how to feel.

But briefly, the film opens onto a stranger world.

It is a world in which people live closer to death. The borders between necessity, ritual, and cruelty are not where I expect them to be.

I wanted the film to remain there a little longer.

When I encounter something unfamiliar, I tend to look for a contemporary equivalent. A god becomes a psychological condition. A monster becomes trauma. A voyage becomes a metaphor for recovering from war.

These translations give me a way into an old story. They can also close the door behind me.

Homer does not always allow that.

His gods are vain, generous, jealous, sexual, petty, and terrifying. They do not behave like symbols. They behave more like weather with personalities.

Human beings live beneath these forces without expecting the universe to be fair or particularly interested in being understood.

I am not sure I would want to live inside that world. I am also not sure I want it cleaned up for me.

There may be value in letting an old thing remain old. In allowing its assumptions to sit beside mine without forcing the two to agree.

The puppy scene carries a little of that feeling. The world does not stop to explain itself. For a moment, I am left without an interpretation.

I would like to remain there for another beat.

Not because the past was wiser, or because strangeness is automatically profound.

I simply like the possibility of meeting an old story without immediately asking it to become me.

To let its gods remain gods. To let the sea remain the sea.
