Good advice can sometimes change the question I’m asking. The *I Ching* has a gift for that. It pays close attention to timing: a course of action can make sense when you begin it and become unwise as the situation changes. [About the *I Ching*](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-original-meaning-of-the-yijing/9780231216609/)

That’s easy enough to appreciate when I’m looking at somebody else’s situation. From a little distance, I can usually see how a reasonable effort became an exhausting commitment. Up close, I’m more likely to keep explaining why I started. The original reasons can remain perfectly sound while the thing I’m doing with them becomes increasingly difficult to defend.

Take Hexagram 6, on conflict. It grants that you can enter a dispute sincerely and still come to grief by pursuing it to the end. That is easy for me to miss in the middle of an argument. I can be right about what happened and completely wrong about what needs to happen next. So I might open the book asking, “How do I get them to admit I’m right?” and walk away asking, “If they admitted it tomorrow, what would that actually repair?” [Hexagram 6, Sòng](https://en.mouseia.org/texts/i-ching/section-6)

An admission might repair quite a lot. It might establish something that needs to be acknowledged before a relationship can continue. But I can also imagine getting the admission and discovering that I’m still unsatisfied. I’ve essentially been asking someone to undo an experience by agreeing with my account of it.

What am I actually trying to recover? Trust? Some practical arrangement? The feeling that my judgment can be relied upon? Each would require something different.

The *I Ching* allows me to spend time with these distinctions. Its hexagrams are figures of six broken or solid lines, accompanied by texts that describe situations, tendencies, difficulties, and possible responses. A reading brings some of those passages into relation with a question. Lines designated as changing produce a second hexagram, giving the reading a sense of movement. [Joseph A. Adler, “Zhu Xi’s Conception of Yijing Divination as Spiritual Practice”](https://www2.kenyon.edu/Depts/Religion/Fac/Adler/Writings/Zhu%20Xi%27s%20Conception%20of%20Divination.pdf) · [My I Ching notes on Keepinghaus](https://keeping.haus/#i-ching)

I read these images against my own circumstances. What counts as “advancing” here? What would waiting involve? Who else is affected? Sometimes a passage fits readily. Sometimes I have to sit with the possibility that I’ve described my situation badly.

Using yarrow stalks to generate a hexagram gives me time to notice what answer I’m hoping for before I open the book. The method involves dividing the stalks, setting some aside, and counting the remaining bundles in groups of four. Three rounds produce one line. It takes eighteen rounds total to form a complete hexagram, so there’s a decent stretch of time when I’ve asked my question and the answer is still taking shape. [Zhu Xi, “Divination Ritual,” in *The Original Meaning of the Yijing*, translated by Joseph A. Adler](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-original-meaning-of-the-yijing/9780231216609/)

Suppose you ask whether a piece of work is ready to show someone. Halfway through counting, you catch yourself hoping the book will say wait. That’s worth noticing. Does the work need another revision, or would another revision spare you the discomfort of showing it? Now you know something about what you brought to the question.

You still have to examine the work. But you’ve caught a preference that could easily have presented itself as judgment.

I find that sort of discovery easier to make when my hands are occupied. Counting gives the time some shape. I have to attend to what I’m doing, and the question stays nearby, maybe even coming back several times and sounding slightly different each time.

In Zhu Xi’s traditional instructions, you address your uncertainty directly to the stalks and ask for guidance. Counting by fours evokes the four seasons. The procedure places a particular human uncertainty within a larger order of change. [Zhu Xi, “Divination Ritual”](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-original-meaning-of-the-yijing/9780231216609/)

That gives the moment a seriousness I appreciate. I have to formulate what I’m asking and acknowledge that I’m uncertain about it. Even saying a question aloud can expose an assumption I’d managed to leave comfortably unexamined. Asking how to make someone agree with me sounds different when I have to hear myself ask it.

Under the conventional probability model for the yarrow method, a solid line changing to a broken one is three times as likely as the reverse. Three fair coins give those changes equal odds. The method affects which changing-line passages I’m likely to encounter. Those traditional probabilities describe an idealized model, and the way a person actually divides the stalks can affect the distribution. [Yarrow probability model](https://yigua.art/about/probability) · [Yarrow and coin probabilities compared](https://www.simonluowei.com/en/iching/simulator/)

I like that the practical details reach all the way into the reading. The time spent counting, the gestures, the possible results, and the words belong to the same activity. There’s something to consider at each stage.

Of course, I can bring all my usual evasions to this, too. I can favor a reassuring translation, stretch an image until it accommodates what I wanted, or become fascinated by the interpretation long enough to postpone doing anything. Wanting guidance gives me plenty of opportunities to arrange for the guidance I want.

So I try to pay attention to my response. Relief is worth examining. So is irritation. If a passage about waiting makes me impatient, perhaps there’s something I’m trying to hurry past. If it makes me enormously relieved, perhaps waiting needs a closer look.

Eventually I put the stalks away. The work is still on the desk. The person I’m angry with still has their own understanding of what happened. I have to decide what to do with both.
