Ted Huntington has apparently spent a large part of his life trying to figure out what is connected to what.

Science, politics, veganism, violence, language, music, the future of humanity, photographs of birds, presidential debates, cosmology, the inner workings of the brain. Follow Ted for very long and the borders between these subjects start getting pretty porous.

His personal site, [tedhuntington.com](https://tedhuntington.com/), has been accumulating this stuff for decades. It isn’t especially interested in helping me decide what matters most. Ideas, images, theories, songs, arguments, observations, citations, and personal artifacts sit close together, often with about the same visual weight.

My first instinct as a designer is to start fixing things.

That instinct is not entirely wrong. The site is difficult to navigate. Hierarchy can disappear. Claims that might deserve very different levels of scrutiny can look strangely equivalent on the page. There are places where the sheer volume of material works against whatever Ted is trying to convince me of.

But “fixing” it too quickly also misses something.

One of Ted’s largest projects, *Universe, Life, Science, Future*, attempts nothing less than a timeline of the universe, evolution, science, human history, and the future. It combines scientific sources, public-domain imagery, Ted’s own diagrams, and his own theories into one enormous sequence. The ambition is almost comically large, but I don’t mean that entirely as criticism. Most of us have been trained not to attempt things this size unless there is a grant, an institution, or at least a reasonably convincing PowerPoint behind us.

Ted just did it.

He has also written books with titles like *Big Science Secrets, Lies, and Mistakes* and *Direct to Brain Windows*, built arguments about science that range from unconventional to claims I find very difficult to take seriously, organized vegan groups, posted dozens of questions for a presidential debate, and recorded environmental and political songs. [A UC Irvine article from 2007](https://news.uci.edu/2007/07/25/kroq-jocks-put-uc-irvine-programmer-analyst-on-the-air/) describes him talking on KROQ about everything from violence to science education after his barrage of YouTube debate questions attracted attention. The article is still there. His music is still coming too; as recently as 2025 he was releasing songs with titles like “Nothing Wrong With Love” and “Beethoven’s 7th Jamphony.” You can listen to them.

This is not somebody who seems particularly burdened by the question of whether the world asked for any of it.

I admire that.

Not because persistence makes his ideas correct. It doesn’t. Some of Ted’s scientific claims seem unsupported or plainly wrong to me, and the site doesn’t always do much to help a skeptical reader distinguish an established source from one of Ted’s own leaps. Putting everything into one giant network can create the impression that connecting two things is the same as demonstrating the connection.

It isn’t.

But there’s another failure hiding inside the success of a more orderly site. Most websites are very good at concealing the mind that made them. Information gets sorted into the proper sections, peculiar interests are removed, tangents are disciplined, and eventually a person with a complicated inner life becomes HOME / ABOUT / WORK / CONTACT.

Ted goes hard in the opposite direction.

His site lets the connections remain visible, including the dubious ones. You can watch one concern run into another. Science turns into politics. Politics turns into violence. Violence turns into ethics. Ethics turns into food. Somewhere along the way somebody takes a photograph of a bluebird.

That makes the site harder to use and, for me, much easier to care about.

I don’t think Ted was necessarily trying to build an intimate portrait of himself. He seems much more interested in persuading people that the things he has discovered are important. But after decades of accumulation, the portrait may be the thing that works best.

I can see the shape of his attention.

There’s generosity in it, even when I don’t know what to do with the conclusions. He appears to believe information should be shared, violence should be reduced, people should participate more directly in their government, animals should suffer less, science should explain more, and that if he has figured something out he has some obligation to tell the rest of us about it. His methods of getting there can be bewildering. The impulse is easier for me to recognize.

And there’s something useful in that recognition.

I spend a fair amount of energy deciding which connections are defensible before I let anyone see them. That is generally a good idea. Editing exists for reasons.

But Ted’s site reminds me that editing has a cost too. If I clean up every strange association before anybody sees it, eventually I may remove the evidence of how I actually got from one thought to another.

Ted Huntington has not made that mistake.

He may have made several others.

But the wires are still showing.
