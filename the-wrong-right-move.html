The Wrong Right Move

Something can work perfectly well and still be wrong for the thing you’re making.

October 5, 2026

5 minute read

Edit

So there I was, writing an essay about technique and taste with my super‑intelligent writing partner, trying to work through why technically accomplished writing can sometimes feel so empty…and we were producing quite a lot of technically accomplished writing in the process.

The argument was fairly simple. Writers develop recognizable techniques. They learn how to structure an argument, when to introduce a joke, how to make a metaphor do some work, when to give a sentence its own paragraph. Eventually these things become part of what we call their voice.

But knowing how to do something isn’t the same as knowing when to do it.

In my mind there’s a relationship here to kitsch. Something can have all the recognizable features of good design and still fail at what it’s supposed to do.

At some point we decided to go back through our draft and look for examples of exactly this problem. We found plenty. There were jokes that existed simply because the essay hadn’t had onein a while. There were metaphors that made already clear ideas more complicated and far too many one sentence paragraphs. So we started taking things out.

What interested me wasn’t so much that the AI had made these choices. That was predictable. What interested me was what happened after I rejected them.

I’d originally given Chat examples of my finished writing, hoping it could learn something about my voice. Unfortunately, finished work leaves out an enormous amount of information. It doesn’t show all the jokes I deleted, the metaphors I abandoned, or the sentences I spent twenty minutes noodling before deciding they weren’t worth keeping.

Now, because we were revising together, it could see some of that. It could see that I’d rejected a particular joke for being too cute, or collapsed a dramatic one‑sentence paragraph back into the surrounding text.

That seemed promising, until I realized there was another problem: the AI could learn those rejections as readily as it had learned my preferences. Instead of writing too many jokes, it could learn to remove jokes. Instead of breaking every third sentence into its own paragraph, it could learn to avoid doing that. And if I told it I preferred humor that emerged naturally from an observation, it could start producing what it recognized as naturally emerging humor.

We were getting better at describing my taste, but I wasn’t sure we were getting any closer to reproducing it. Adapting without comprehending.

This is what makes voice imitation so difficult. Give a model twenty essays by the same writer and it will identify recurring characteristics. Perhaps the writer is funny, likes unusual comparisons, or tends to approach large ideas through small personal experiences. These are perfectly reasonable observations.

And of course writers do this to themselves. I certainly do. Once I’ve found a way of writing that people respond to, it’s tempting to keep reaching for it. I start recognizing opportunities to sound like myself. Sometimes that means I’m becoming more confident in my voice. Sometimes it means I’m repeating an old trick.

AI did not invent this problem. Honestly, it can imitate my mannerisms so quickly and convincingly that I start noticing how often I rely on them myself. It can feel like working with an impersonator rather than a collaborator.

There is also something strange about watching a model learn from your corrections. It can become more restrained, more subtle, less eager to perform. The resulting prose might be considerably better. But—of course—restraint can become a mannerism too. An essay can be just as formulaic in its refusal to be clever as in its attempts to be clever.

So I’m not sure the answer is a better list of stylistic rules. At some point, the list starts looking suspiciously like another formula.

What seems more useful at the moment is to take away a technique temporarily and see what happens. Remove the joke. Replace the metaphor with a plain description. Put the isolated sentence back where it came from. If the passage loses something important, put it back. You’ve learned what the technique was contributing. If nothing much changes, perhaps you liked the move more than you needed it.

It is not a perfect test. Some things are worth keeping simply because they’re pleasurable, and pleasure is a perfectly legitimate reason to write a sentence a certain way. Not every stylistic choice needs a functional justification. But there’s a difference between enjoying something and using it out of habit, and I don’t always find that difference easy to recognize.

So…I began this essay thinking about a limitation of AI writing. Somewhere in the middle I found myself thinking about my own habits instead.

I still don’t know how much of what we call taste can be taught to a model at the moment. Maybe a sufficiently detailed history of someone’s choices, including everything they rejected, would get surprisingly close. Perhaps there’s something about encountering a particular subject, at a particular moment, that can’t be recovered from those patterns.

What I do know is that giving the model more rules doesn’t settle the question. It can learn what I usually do and what I usually avoid, but I still have to decide what this particular piece needs.
